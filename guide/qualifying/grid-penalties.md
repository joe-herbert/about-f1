A grid penalty means a driver starts further back than where they qualified. A five-place grid penalty for someone who qualified third would, in the simplest case, see them start eighth. The penalty is always applied to the starting grid, so it doesn't affect the qualifying result itself.

Grid penalties apply to either the sprint or the main race, depending on which one the stewards attach them to. Penalties for using too many power unit parts always apply to the main race.

## Why drivers get grid penalties

### Using too many power unit parts

This is the most common reason. Each driver can only use a set number of each part of the [power unit](/guide/car/power-unit/) during the season. Going over the limit costs grid places.

The 2026 limits per driver are below. These include an extra unit of each that every driver gets for 2026 only, because the power units are brand new this year.

| Power unit part | Allowed in 2026 |
| --- | --- |
| Engine (ICE) | 4 |
| Turbocharger (TC) | 4 |
| Exhaust sets | 4 |
| MGU-K (the electric motor) | 3 |
| Energy store (the battery) | 3 |
| Control electronics | 3 |

The penalties are:

- **10 places** the first time a driver uses an extra part of a given type
- **5 places** for each extra part of that type after that

The penalties add up. Fitting a fifth engine and a fourth MGU-K at the same weekend means two 10-place penalties, 20 places in all. A new part counts as "used" as soon as the car leaves the pit lane with it fitted, even if it only does one lap.

From 2027 the bonus unit goes away, so the limits drop to 3 engines, turbos and exhaust sets, and 2 of each of the electrical parts.

### Penalties from the stewards

The [stewards](/guide/penalties/the-stewards/) can hand out a grid drop for something that happened earlier in the weekend, or at the previous race. Common examples are:

- **Impeding** another driver in qualifying, usually 3 places
- Causing a collision in the previous race
- Driving unsafely in practice, such as ignoring a red flag
- A time penalty that couldn't be served because the driver retired from the race, which the stewards can turn into a grid drop at the next race

### Five reprimands

A [reprimand](/guide/penalties/types-of-penalties/) on its own has no immediate effect. But a driver who gets five in a season, at least four of them for driving offences, gets an automatic 10-place grid penalty. If the fifth comes during a race, the penalty is served at the next event.

## Going to the back of the grid

If a driver's grid penalties add up to more than 15 places, they start from the back, behind every driver who qualified normally. If several drivers are sent to the back, they line up in the order they qualified.

This is why teams often take several new power unit parts at once. A driver already going to the back loses nothing extra by taking a whole fresh set, and that gives them spare parts for later races.

There's a limit to that trick, though. If a driver fits more than one of the same part at a single weekend, only the last one fitted can be used at later races without a further penalty. Teams can't build up a stockpile in one go.

## How the grid is worked out

When several drivers have penalties, the regulations use a set process so everyone ends up in a fair order:

1. Each penalised driver (15 places or less) is given a temporary position: their qualifying position plus their penalty.
2. Drivers without penalties fill the remaining slots in qualifying order.
3. Penalised drivers move forward to fill any gaps left over.
4. Drivers with more than 15 places of penalties go to the back, in qualifying order.
5. Anyone who wasn't classified in qualifying but has been allowed to race starts behind all of them.

Because of step 3, a driver can end up losing fewer places than their penalty suggests. If lots of drivers behind them also have penalties, there's nobody to swap with. At heavily penalised races this happens a lot, and the final grid can look very different from the qualifying order.

## Strategy behind engine penalties

Teams don't usually wait for a part to wear out. They plan when to take a penalty, choosing tracks where overtaking is easier so the driver can recover. Long straights and wide braking zones, at places like Monza or Spa-Francorchamps, are popular choices. A team might also take a penalty at a weekend where the driver has already had a poor qualifying, so they lose less.

> Earlier versions of the rules also limited gearboxes, with their own grid penalties. The 2026 sporting regulations only set grid penalties for the power unit parts listed above.

If a driver also has to [start from the pit lane](/guide/qualifying/pit-lane-starts/), for example because their team changed the car while it was in [parc fermé](/guide/qualifying/parc-ferme/), the grid penalty makes little practical difference, since they're starting behind everyone anyway.
